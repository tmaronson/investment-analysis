This application is an investment analysis starting with a mix of random stocks and bonds downloaded from yfinance with a ratio of stocks to bonds of 73% : 27% and with 20 years of data. The data is cleaned  Initially volatility The initial portfolio value is normalized to one million dollars ($1,000,000). A Monte Carlo simulation is used to simulate 10,000 random paths. Gross median wealth and net median wealth are calculated over a 20 year time horizon. The net return is based on a 60 basis (0.6%) point management fee. Gross and net median returns are calculated as are annualized volatility and annualized returns.

Plot fandown charts with Matplotlib and Seaborn between 5th and 95th percentile. The 95th percentile represents a top five percent 20 year economic scenario across ten thousand simulated scenarios, while the 5th percentile represents the bottom five percent market cycle. The spread between the 5th and 95th percentiles expands exponentially over time for both cases due to market volatility. The gross confidence band stretches even higher in raw dollars because higher compounding drives top-tail wealth exponentially. Exponential and not linear growth is seen clearly on the graphs. The fee drag does steadily pull down the entire net distribution, shifting the median lower and compressing your upside across every scenario.

Plot same idea on a histogram now. Clustering right in the six to ten percent annualized corridor is the central limit theorem and geometric compounding working exactly as expected. When you simulate twenty consecutive years drawn from a distribution with an annualized mean near 9.8% and a 14% volatility (as calculated above from these simulations), the law of large numbers pulls the vast majority of multi-decade paths toward that center. For a portfolio to end up in an extreme tail, like averaging negative returns over 20 years or compounding at 20% every single year -- you would need an long and unrealistic run of catastrophic depressions or an unprecedented multi-decade boom. Over a 20 year horizon, good years and bad years naturally balance out, concentrating the probability mass right in that central band.
This is like flipping a coin 10 thousand times. Randomly simulating coin flips as two possibilities, heads or tails naturally, will give a very close approximation to what we expect from probablility - 5000 heads and 5000 tails. Increasing the number of flips will get even closer to 50% for heads and tails.
That is also why visualizing the histogram is so powerful when thinking about fees. Many people dismiss sixty basis points because they feel market volatility is so large that sixty basis points gets lost in the noise. But market volatility is symmetric and fluctuates over time, whereas an advisory fee is a deterministic drag on growth, kind of like static friction on a moving object in Physics. Within that dense 6 - 10% return band where most realistic futures live, subtracting 60 basis points is taking a permanent 6 - 10% bite out of your total annual growth rate every single year. When compounded over twenty years, that constant leftward shift in the bell curve is what creates that gap of about $600,000 for the initial normalized portfolio of $1 million.
